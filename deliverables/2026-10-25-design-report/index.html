Where Jack comes from

A head that already works, looking for a body

Jack's head was built in 2023: printed orange face, glowing blue eyes, blinking eyelids, a talking jaw, and a neck that turns and nods. A camera in his nose finds faces so he can look at whoever walks up, and he has a few dozen cowboy voice lines. This project gives him a torso, two arms and hands, and a battery so he can stand on his own all evening.

The three versions

Pick how much Jack can do

All three share the same head, base, costume and battery system. They differ in the arms, the hands and how much they can sense.

What every Jack has

How he stands, and what powers him

A bucket of concrete for feet

Jack has no legs. He stands on a steel pipe set into a 5-gallon bucket filled with concrete, about 44 kg, sitting on a 24-inch plywood disc that hides under his costume. The pipe screws into a floor flange cast into the concrete.

One battery system

He runs on the Milwaukee M18 packs we already own. A dock with a switch and a fuse takes the pack. A cut-off module switches everything off before the pack runs flat, because an M18 pack can't be relied on to protect itself without a Milwaukee tool attached. Converters then make the voltages each part needs.

A big red stop button

A latching emergency stop on his back cuts power to every motor at once, while the computer stays on. In tiers B and C the button holds a heavy-duty relay, so it also stops the hands.

How long he runs

A 12 Ah pack holds about 216 watt-hours. We will measure Jack's real power draw on the bench. Until then, here are planning figures (assumed draws, 85% converter efficiency):

If Jack averages12 Ah pack8 Ah pack
30 W (mostly idle)~6 h~4 h
50 W (regular gestures)~3.7 h~2.4 h
80 W (busy all evening)~2.3 h~1.5 h

Tiers B and C swap packs without stopping, and tier C can also run from the wall.

Keeping him upright

In the simulation, a push at shoulder height with both arms out tips him at 154–164 N (about 16–17 kg of force). A teenager leaning on his shoulder could do that.

For events: lay two 25 lb sandbags on the plywood disc under the costume: the simulation then needs 205–215 N, about a third more. Or tie the pole to a post.

How he works

Eight hobby servos, the kind used in RC cars: at each shoulder two 35 kg·cm servos, at the elbow a 25 kg·cm one, each driving its joint through a 3:1 toothed belt, plus one in each mitt. A second servo board joins the one already in the head, so the head's computer runs everything. The torso is a box of 12 mm birch plywood on the steel pole.

What he can do

  • Waves: lifts his arm forward and up and waves from the elbow
  • Grips a light candy bag with a one-motor mitt
  • Shoulder springs carry the arm's weight, so the small motors only move it
  • Runs on one M18 pack with a low-battery cut-off
  • Big red stop button on his back

What he can't

  • Feel when he touches someone (no feedback from the motors)
  • Twist his upper arm or wrist
  • Swap the battery without switching off

Tier B · was up to $1,200

Midrange

The one to build for a season of events: smart motors, safer, and he never has to sleep.

$1,381new parts

How he works

Fourteen Feetech STS3215 smart servos, the motors used in popular open-source robot arms. At each shoulder and elbow the servo drives a hollow hub on two thin bearings through a 3:1 toothed belt; the upper-arm and wrist twists are driven directly. A spring-and-cable balancer at each shoulder carries the arm's weight. An aluminium frame in the torso carries two Milwaukee packs, joined so one can be swapped live.

What he can do

  • Waves properly: the upper arm twists, so the hand comes up palm out
  • Glove hands that open and close around a cup or a bag handle
  • Every motor reports its position, load and temperature
  • Two battery packs: pull one while the other keeps him going
  • Tells you when the battery is getting low

What he can't

  • Move individual fingers
  • Hold an arm straight out to the side for long (shown in the simulation below)

The show piece: real robot hands with fingers that each move on their own.

$2,370new parts

How he works

Tier B's arm, upgraded: stronger 50 kg·cm STS3250 motors at both shoulder joints and the elbow (still through belts), plus a wrist that bends. Each hand is a Pollen Robotics AmazingHand: an open-source hand with eight tiny motors in the palm. A Raspberry Pi 5 in the chest runs the arms and hands. A mains supply joins the two battery packs.

What he can do

  • Humanoid hands: four fingers, each with two moving joints
  • A wrist that bends as well as twists, for a hat-tip like the concept art
  • Batteries plus wall power, so he can run all day indoors
  • His own computer in the chest for the arms and hands

What he can't

  • Thumbs (the hand used has four fingers, which suits a cartoon)
  • Strong grip: the hand's grip force is not published

210 parts · 614 screws, nuts and inserts · 43 cables · 2.4 kg of printed parts

Before we build

What still needs a decision or a test

Decisions for Mark

  1. Which tier to build. Recommended: B, with C's hands as a later upgrade.
  2. Sign the design brief (it sets what "done" means: runtime per pack, tip test, gesture list).
  3. What Jack should hold: we assumed a 0.25 kg candy bag.
  4. Gestures: keep arms raised forward rather than straight out sideways (the simulation's one limit), or add a second spring at the shoulder-out joint.
  5. Indoor only, or outdoors too? Rain changes the electronics enclosure.
  6. Is the overall height (about 1.7 m with the hat) right?

Bench tests before the first event

  1. Set each shoulder and elbow servo's torque limit (the simulation's belt limit) before the first power-up.
  2. Weigh the head (assumed 2 kg in the tip check).
  3. Measure average power with the arms moving, which sets the real runtime.
  4. Check the stop relay switches on reliably from a part-drained pack (tiers B and C).
  5. Set the low-battery cut-off from the cell datasheet (about 15–16 V).
  6. Tip test with sandbags, and a pinch check at every joint.

Things we found in the existing head

Its code sets two pins by physical number while using the chip's own pin numbering, so the "power" pins for the neck limit switch may not exist. One file imports a misspelled library name, and voice lines use paths from the old computer. None of this affects the body design, but they are worth fixing when the software is next opened.

How sure are these numbers?

Prices were looked up on 24 Oct 2026, mostly from store listings, and will move; quantities come straight from the 3D model, packs bought whole. A few items (some wire gauges, an M5 tap) are not priced yet. The simulation uses the model's weights and the makers' motor ratings; the hobby servos publish no continuous rating, so we assumed a third of their maximum. The belt rating is the belt maker's figure. The head's 2 kg is an assumption until weighed. Nothing has been ordered.
