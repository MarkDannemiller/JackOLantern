## 2. What all three tiers share

**Base and mast.** A 5 US gal bucket (18.9 L) of concrete is about 43 kg when
full (*estimate*, 2.3 kg/L). A steel pipe mast is set in it (threaded pipe into
a floor flange cast in the concrete, or a pipe with a cross-bolt), with a
height-adjust clamp at the torso. **Stability needs checking:** about the
bucket rim (radius about 0.15 m) the ballast resists about 63 N·m; a person
leaning on Jack's shoulder with 50 N at 1.2 m is already 60 N·m (*estimate,
assumed push*). Proposal: the bucket sits in a wide skirt or base disc (0.6 m
across roughly doubles the resisting moment), hidden under the costume.
Recompute with real masses at level 3.

**Torso.** A frame on the mast carries the shoulders, the head mount on top,
and the electronics bay. Battery dock behind a rear hatch so a pack swaps
without taking the costume off. The costume or shell gives the character look.

**Power.** Milwaukee M18 (18 V nominal, 5 cells). An adapter dock gives
battery leads; the pack is fused, switched, and protected by a low-voltage
cutoff (do not rely on the pack: confirm in research what an M18 pack does
without a tool's electronics). From the 18 V bus, converters give 5 V for the
Pi and the servo or actuator rail. The head moves its power into the torso
too, so one battery runs everything. Runtime = pack Wh x converter efficiency
/ average draw. Example only: a 12 Ah pack is 216 Wh; at an *assumed* 50 W
average that is about 3.7 h at 85 % efficiency. The real average draw is
**TBD: measured on the bench** per tier.

**Safety (it stands among trick-or-treaters).** A latching emergency-stop
button that cuts actuator power while the Pi stays up; speed and joint
limits in software; arms whose rest pose hangs on hard stops so a power cut
drops nothing; no pinch points in the hand or at the elbow (covered gaps);
grip force limited by design. Tiers B and C add load sensing to stop on
contact.

**Software.** Extend the existing `MotionController` with arm and hand
channels and a small library of gestures (wave, point, beckon, offer the
candy bowl, shrug) timed to the voice lines. Face tracking can aim a wave
or a point at the visitor.

**Arm sizing (applies to all, estimate).** Assumed cartoon proportions:
upper arm 0.25 m, forearm 0.22 m, hand 0.12 m; masses 0.45 / 0.45 / 0.25 kg;
payload 0.25 kg (a candy bag). Holding the arm straight out needs about
**4.7 N·m (48 kg·cm) at the shoulder** and **1.9 N·m (19 kg·cm) at the
elbow**, before margin. That is past what a hobby servo holds for hours,
so every tier puts a **spring counterbalance on shoulder flex** (it carries
the steady gravity load, the actuator only the motion) and favours poses
with the arms down or bent between gestures.

## 3. The three tiers

Budget cap = new spend on body, arms, hands and power; the existing head is
reused and not counted (Mark, 2026-10-24).

### A. Beginner: under $500

| | |
|---|---|
| Arms | 4 joints each: shoulder flex, shoulder out (abduction), elbow, plus the hand. 8 actuators total |
| Actuators | High-torque hobby PWM servos (35 kg·cm class, metal gear); shoulder flex geared 2:1 with a printed or belt stage plus the spring |
| Control | Existing Pi; a second PCA9685 (I2C address 0x41) for the arm channels |
| Hands | Cartoon mitt: 3 fingers moulded as one piece closing on a thumb, 1 servo, TPU pads. Holds a bag handle or a light bowl |
| Power | One M18 adapter dock with fuse and switch, a 5 V buck for the Pi, one high-current buck for the servos (servo voltage TBD by the servo picked), e-stop on the servo rail |
| Structure | Bucket + concrete + steel pipe mast; plywood torso frame cut on our laser; printed arm links (PLA or PETG); fabric or foam costume |
| Gives up | No position or load feedback (servos hum while holding, cannot sense contact); single battery, so a swap powers it down |
| Allocation | servos ~45 %, power ~20 %, structure ~20 %, filament, fasteners, costume ~15 % |

### B. Midrange: up to $1200

| | |
|---|---|
| Arms | 6 joints each: shoulder flex, shoulder out, upper-arm twist, elbow, wrist twist, plus the hand. 12 actuators total |
| Actuators | 12 V serial bus servos (Feetech STS series, the actuator of the open-source SO-100/SO-101 arm): position, load, temperature and voltage readback on one daisy-chained cable. Shoulder joints on a higher-torque model and/or a 3:1 belt stage, plus the spring |
| Control | Existing Pi plus a USB bus-servo adapter. Load readback gives stall and contact detection and soft compliant holding |
| Hands | Character glove hand: 4 cartoon fingers linked to close together (underactuated linkage, so they wrap around an object) against a separately driven thumb. 2 actuators per hand |
| Power | **Two M18 docks with ideal-diode OR-ing**: pull a pack while the other runs, no reboot. Per-branch fuses, a voltage and current monitor (Jack can say he is getting sleepy before the pack runs out), e-stop relay on the actuator rail |
| Structure | Aluminium extrusion torso frame, PETG printed arm links and shells, laser-cut panels; same base |
| Allocation | actuators ~50 %, power and monitoring ~20 %, structure ~20 %, finish ~10 % |

### C. Maxed: humanoid hands

| | |
|---|---|
| Arms | 7 joints each (adds wrist bend), on higher-torque bus servos with metal horns and bearings at every joint (Dynamixel X-series class), or B's arm upgraded |
| Hands | Real humanoid hands: 4 or 5 fingers, each finger driven, 6 to 11 actuators per hand, tendon or linkage driven from the forearm. Candidates to evaluate in research: Pollen Robotics' open-source AmazingHand (4 fingers, which suits a cartoon character), the InMoov hand (5 tendon fingers, servos in the forearm), the LEAP Hand (16 joints, Dynamixel, higher cost), or a commercial hand. Fingertip force sensing optional |
| Control | Pi 5 or a Jetson-class computer for perception, and a motion co-processor for the hand and arm servo bus timing; a puppeteer mode (a passive leader arm or a glove records gestures that Jack plays back) |
| Power | B's dual-M18 hot swap, plus a 24 V wall-supply input on the same OR-ing for indoor all-day runs. A **custom power distribution board** designed in the stack (`circuit`): ideal-diode OR, electronic fuses per branch, e-stop, monitoring over I2C |
| Structure | B's frame, with shells sized to hide the larger hand actuators in the forearm |
| Budget | **TBD: no cap given (Q3).** Hands dominate the cost |

## 4. How it is built (hub phases, stack levels)

1. **Research (now).** Answer the open questions (`research/questions.md`),
   get the head CAD, verify the M18 adapter and pack protection, the actuator
   families and the hand candidates. Exit: Mark signs the brief.
2. **Design in `protoboard/`**, one stack level at a time, asking before each:
   - Level 1, concept: five blocks (Power, Control, Head (existing), Arms x2,
     Structure and ballast) for all three tiers, since they share it.
   - Level 2, blocks: each block opened, interfaces with voltages and bus
     protocols, bought or designed here. This is where the tiers separate.
   - Level 3, parts: real parts from the Protoboard catalog
     (`protoboard parts search`), else researched into `parts/local/`;
     `protoboard bom` prices each tier against its cap. Harnesses: battery to
     power, power to Pi and actuators, torso to head through the neck, the
     shoulder-to-hand cable runs. G-PLAN signs here.
   - Level 4, verified: part verification, sourcing audit, fault scenarios,
     an independent review.
   - Level 5, build files: torso plates and brackets (`frame-cad`), the
     electronics bay (`enclosure-cad`), cables (`harness-cad`), Tier C's
     power board (`circuit`), the release.
3. **Manufacturing.** We print and laser-cut in-house; a person orders and
   presses go.
4. **Testing.** Bench power draw (sets the real runtime), joint temperatures
   over a long run, tip test, pinch and e-stop checks, then a full-evening
   soak test.

Suggested order of building: Tier A first as the working prototype of the
shared parts (base, mast, torso, power, gestures). Tiers B and C reuse them and
change the arm, hand and power-board blocks.

**Where the stack falls short here** (to log as friction as it is met):
the stack has one `root` per project and no notion of product variants, so
three tiers need a choice (Q6); and it models structure and electrical
connections but not joint kinematics or torque, so the arm sizing lives in
a calculation script beside the model in `protoboard/docs/`.
